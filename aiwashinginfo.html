What is AI washing?

AI washing means making misleading or exaggerated claims about a business’s use of artificial intelligence or what its AI can achieve. This can include advertising capabilities that do not exist, misrepresenting how much work is automated, or making performance claims without adequate evidence. The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) explicitly used the term in its 2024 enforcement communications. Read the SEC’s explanation.

Patterns illustrated by the cases below

  • Misrepresented AI use: the advertised AI function is absent or materially different from the actual service.
  • Hidden human work: claims of autonomy conceal substantial manual processing.
  • Inflated performance: accuracy claims extend beyond the conditions in which a tool was tested.
  • Unsupported promises: claims of professional expertise, freedom from bias, or automatic compliance exceed the evidence.

Human oversight and third-party AI can be legitimate parts of a product. Assess whether the company describes its capabilities accurately and can support its claims.

AI washing in stock markets

There are two distinct exposures: buying shares in a company that overstates its AI business, and trusting an adviser who misrepresents how AI manages investments. Private startup fundraising is a third category and is identified separately in the dataset.

  1. 1. A capability claimManagement promises automation, proprietary technology, or an AI trading advantage.
  2. 2. A financial assumptionInvestors may infer faster growth, lower labor costs, or a defensible competitive advantage.
  3. 3. A valuation riskIf the claim is unsupported, projected revenue, margins, or returns may need revision.

Listed businesses: what is the AI really worth?

Presto misstated human involvement in its voice ordering system. That matters when evaluating whether a product can scale without proportional labor costs. Kubient is a stronger evidentiary case: its former CEO pleaded guilty over fabricated revenue and misrepresentations about its AI tool, then was sentenced in March 2025.

Evolv illustrates another route: unsupported product-performance claims can expose a public business to customer remedies and disputes. These records do not establish a specific share-price loss caused solely by AI washing.

Investment services: is AI doing the trading?

Delphia, Global Predictions, and Rimar settled SEC findings concerning misleading AI claims. They are advisory cases, not three examples of listed AI stocks.

A January 2026 complaint against Joel Sofia / WOLO Wealth alleges purported AI trading software was not used as represented and claims of eliminating downside risk were false. The conduct described predates 2026; the complaint is a new 2026 event.

How AI washing can affect society

The pathways below are an analysis of risks illustrated by the cited cases. Their scale has not been measured across society.

  1. 1. Unsupported promiseA product appears more capable, autonomous, or reliable than the evidence supports.
  2. 2. Decision based on that promiseA buyer spends money, an investor allocates capital, or an organization reduces human review.
  3. 3. Costs reach peopleFinancial loss, inaccessible services, unreliable decisions, and additional correction work can follow.

Money and opportunity

Investors and small businesses can commit scarce funds based on inflated capabilities or earnings promises. Misleading claims can also draw business away from providers that substantiate their results.

Evidence: investment-adviser findings and Air AI allegations and settlement record.

Access and equal treatment

Buyers may assume accessibility barriers have been removed or that a system works equally well for everyone. Unsupported assurances can discourage testing that would reveal exclusion or uneven error rates.

Evidence: accessiBe and IntelliVision.

Trust and consequential decisions

Overstated expertise can encourage people to rely on a tool for decisions it has not been shown to handle. For example, treating a chatbot as a qualified lawyer or a detector score as definitive evidence can expose users to avoidable mistakes.

Evidence: DoNotPay and Workado. These are potential downstream risks, not findings that every such harm occurred.

Work and accountability

Concealing manual work prevents customers and investors from understanding who performs a service and how automation might scale. The Nate allegations illustrate this transparency problem; they do not establish that AI washing caused a particular level of job loss.

Evidence: DOJ’s Nate indictment announcement.

Identify recurring AI-washing patterns

Expand each pattern to connect a warning sign with an evidence check. A warning sign is a reason to investigate, not proof of misconduct.

1. AI capability that cannot be demonstrated

Example: Delphia’s advertised capabilities. Check: Ask for a demonstration of the deployed feature, its inputs, and records showing it is used in the promised process. A future roadmap is different from an available capability.

2. Autonomy claims that omit manual work

Example: the Nate allegations. Check: Define a completed task and count human intervention across all tasks, including failures and exceptions. Human assistance is compatible with an accurately described AI service.

3. A narrow test presented as universal accuracy

Example: Workado’s accuracy claim. Check: Match the evaluation data to the intended users and content. Ask for error rates and test conditions as well as a headline percentage.

4. Absolute promises about expertise, fairness, or compliance

Examples: DoNotPay, IntelliVision, and accessiBe. Check: Ask what the claim means operationally, which situations are covered, and what independent evaluation supports it.

5. AI branding attached to unsupported earnings promises

Example: Air AI’s disputed business-growth claims. Check: Examine typical earnings after costs and the actual refund conditions. AI branding does not establish that a business opportunity is profitable.

Questions to ask about an AI claim

A practical checklist based on the patterns in these cases:

  1. What does the AI actually do? Identify the specific function and whether customers can use it today.
  2. Where do humans intervene? Ask how often staff complete, review, or correct the work.
  3. What supports the performance claim? Look for test methods, sample sizes, error rates, and independent evaluations.
  4. Do the tests match the intended use? Results on one type of content or population may not transfer to another.
  5. What benefit was measured? Compare results with the previous process, including review effort and costs.
  6. How strong is the evidence? Distinguish marketing, independent testing, regulatory allegations, settlements, and court findings.

Limits of this overview: These selected cases show that misleading AI claims occur across industries. They do not measure how common AI washing is or establish which industry has the highest rate.
